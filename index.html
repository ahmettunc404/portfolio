About Me

I’m a student at Saskatchewan Polytechnic, and currently studying Computer Systems Technology. My journey into tech began on a repair bench, taking apart dead phones and laptops, tracing signals through schematics, and finding out why a board failed. I still do that work at Nanotech Mobile Repair, where I've learned to diagnose and fix problems that I'm greatful for.

That hardware background pulled me toward software. I write C/C++ on Linux with Git, I earned my CCNA in 2025, and at the repair bench I work with interfaces like PCIe and I2C. I like knowing a system end to end, from the circuit up to the code running on it. At school I co-developed a website that teaches Indigenous language and culture to youth.

I don’t quit on a problem. When I hit something unfamiliar, like an unknown board or a protocol I haven’t used, I keep going until I find the root cause.

I also serve as an Infantry Soldier in the Canadian Armed Forces Reserve, where clear communication and small-team trust aren’t optional. I train and compete at MBF Boxing Club, and in my downtime I read biographies of people who built things that lasted.

What I'm Working Toward

I want to work in software that sits close to the hardware: systems and networking code that other things depend on. Right now I’m focused on learning as much as I can, so I’m looking for a team with people better than me, where I can take on real work early and improve fast. Over time I want to become someone who can be trusted with a whole system, from how it’s built to how it runs.

Indigenous Language Learning Platform

HTML/CSSJavaScriptGitTeam Project

What It Is

Indigenous youth in Saskatchewan don’t have many engaging digital tools for learning their languages. Our team at Saskatchewan Polytechnic built a Duolingo-style web app to change that, with interactive lessons and quizzes that teach language and culture together.

I built the front end: the quiz interface, the UI layout, and the JavaScript for scoring and progression. We worked in Git, held regular stand-ups, and revised after each sprint. We finished with a working prototype and presented it to faculty and classmates.

What I Learned

This was the first time I worked on a team where code had to integrate across multiple contributors. I learned that clear commit messages and small, reviewable pull requests matter just as much as the code itself.

Where It Points

Explaining technical decisions to people who aren’t technical is a skill I use now. Whatever I build next, whether it’s systems or networking software, I want it to be easy for the people relying on it to understand and use.

Home Lab & Network Infrastructure

NetworkingLinuxVLANsBashDocker

What It Is

While studying for my CCNA my interest grew, I wanted a real network to practice on, not only simulations, and a place to host a few personal services securely. I built a segmented home network. A managed switch keeps IoT, lab and personal traffic in separate VLANs, and a Linux server runs Pi-hole for DNS filtering and Samba for file sharing in Docker containers. Bash scripts handle nightly backups and uptime monitoring. When something breaks, I work it out from the logs and packet captures.

What I Learned

Configuring a VLAN on paper is easy. Working out at 1 AM why two VLANs can suddenly reach each other is where it sticks. It taught me to change one thing at a time when troubleshooting, and I now keep a changelog of every config change so I can retrace my steps.

Board-Level Diagnostic Workflow

HardwareSchematicsI²C / PCIeDocumentation

What It Is

At Nanotech Mobile Repair, plenty of devices come in with faults a part swap won’t fix: dead power rails, unresponsive touch controllers, boot loops with no visible damage. I've learnt to read manufacturer schematics and board-view diagrams, then use a multimeter and microscope to follow power paths, check voltages on PCIe and I²C lines, and find the failing component. Every fault I solve goes into a personal reference guide: symptom, root cause, fix.

What I Learned

Hardware debugging works like software debugging. You form a hypothesis, test it, and narrow it down. I also learned to write things down. Without the guide, I’d keep solving the same problems from scratch.

Where It Points

Working at the hardware-software boundary means I understand what’s happening below the code I write. Next I want to learn JTAG debugging and get hands-on with development boards like STM32.
